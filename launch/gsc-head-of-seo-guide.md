Part 1. Hire your head of SEO
Step 1: Set up the property the right way
If your site isn't in Search Console yet, add it as a Domain property, not a URL-prefix one. A Domain property covers every version of the site (with and without www, http and https, every subdomain), so nothing goes missing from the numbers
Verification is a DNS record your domain host adds in two minutes. Then submit your sitemap under Sitemaps
Two settings people miss:
The date range. The default is 3 months. For anything strategic, look at 16 months, the maximum. Seasonality and slow trends only show up over a year
Search type. Leave it on Web for this system. Images, Video and News are separate worlds
Then add every person who touches the site as a user, with the right level. Your developer needs to see indexing problems. Your writer needs to see what each page ranks for. Nobody needs your password
-> Domain property, sitemap submitted, 16 months for strategy, everybody on the team with their own access
Step 2: Learn the four numbers and the one filter that matters
The Performance report shows four numbers. Here's how to read each one without fooling yourself:
Clicks. People who came to your site from Google. The only one that pays the bills
Impressions. Times you showed up in the results, whether anyone saw you or not. An impression at position 85 is not a view
CTR. Clicks divided by impressions. Only meaningful for one query and one page at a time
Average position. Read across a whole site, it lies more than any other number in the report. Pin one URL and one query, and only then believe it
Now the filter. Click "Add filter", choose Query, then "Queries not containing", and type your brand name
That's the brand filter, and it changes everything. On chatseo.app, the whole site says 44.8K clicks. Without our brand name, the last 3 months say 3.52K clicks from 789K impressions. The first number is a business doing well. The second number is the SEO, and it's the one you can grow
Run every job below with the brand filter on, unless the job says otherwise. People typing your name are a result of everything else you do. They're not a target
-> Four numbers, read one page and one query at a time. Filter your brand out before you draw any conclusion
Step 3: Give your AI the data, not the dashboard
Your AI assistant can't click around Search Console. You bring the data to it
The simple way: Export. In the Performance report, set the filters, click Export, choose CSV. You get queries, pages, countries, devices and dates as separate tables. Drop the file into your AI chat or project
The better way, if you do this weekly: connect Search Console to your AI through a tool that reads it live, so the AI pulls exactly the rows it needs. Same data, no manual export
Either way, one rule saves you a lot: filter before you send. Don't paste 1,000 rows and ask "what should I do". Send the 30 rows the job needs, with the filter already applied. The AI reads faster, guesses less, and you can check its work
Keep the exports in one folder, named by date and filter:
 
Dated files matter more than they look. In three months, when you judge a change, you'll need the numbers from the day you made it
-> Bring filtered exports to your AI, not screenshots of the dashboard. Date every file, you'll need the before

Part 2. The 10 jobs it hands out every week
Each job below is the same shape: what to open, which filter to run, what decision it leads to, and the prompt to give your AI with the export
Do them in this order. The first ones are the cheapest progress you'll ever get
Job 1: The quick-win list
Open: Performance, Queries, last 3 months, brand filtered out, all four metrics ticked
Sort by impressions. Look at the position column. You're hunting for queries between position 5 and 20 with real impressions: the bottom of page one and the top of page two
This is where SEO pays the fastest. Google already thinks your page is relevant. It just isn't the best answer yet. A few internal links, the keyword in the title, a section that answers the question better, and the page moves
Here's what it looks like on chatseo.app, searches containing "alternative", last 3 months:
 
Every one of these sits between position 7 and 10. "similarweb alternative" alone made 9,571 impressions for 50 clicks. Moving that from 8th to 3rd is worth more than any new article we could write this month
When a single URL collects a pile of impressions on a single query, Google is telling you it already half-trusts you there. No other signal in the report is that strong
The prompt:
 
When you get the list, check each page's position with an exact filter before acting: Query filter "Exact query", plus Page filter "Exact URL". The site-wide position column can blend several pages
-> Positions 5 to 20 with impressions first. Google already half-trusts those pages
Job 2: The title rewrite list
Open: Performance, Queries + Pages, brand filtered out
This time you're looking for pages already in the top 5, with lots of impressions, and a CTR that's low for their position. People see you and pick someone else
The fix is almost always the title. It's the one on-page element that matters most, and the one people write last
What a title that wins the click looks like:
The search, first, word for word
Then one promise shaped by what the searcher wants: a count for a list, a proof for a provider, a scope for a guide
The current year on comparisons, lists and "best" searches
"Best" when it fits. It also catches the "best X" questions people now ask AI assistants
Measured in pixels, not characters: about 600 pixels before Google cuts it
One more source for the title: the queries your page gets lots of impressions for but few clicks. The best one of those often belongs in the title next to the main keyword
The prompt:
 
Then change ONE title, write the date in your log, and leave it alone. A title change shows in the results in a day or two. Its effect on clicks needs weeks to read
-> Top 5 with a low CTR is a title problem. Query first, one promise, measured in pixels
Job 3: The page-two giants
Open: Performance, Pages, last 3 months, brand filtered out, sorted by impressions
Now look for pages with huge impressions and an average position between 11 and 20. These are pages Google shows to a big audience, on page two, where almost nobody clicks
Here's the most painful one on chatseo.app right now:
 
Our free Ahrefs alternative page: 278,764 impressions over the last quarter, sitting around position 17.9, and only 21 clicks. A huge audience is waiting on page one, and we're not on it yet
A rewrite won't fix this. When a page sits on page two for a competitive search, what usually decides it is links: links from your own related pages first, then links from other sites straight to that page
The order matters:
1. Internal links. At least 3 to 5 from your pages on the same topic, inside the text, with the page's exact keyword as the anchor. Free, and the cheapest lever in SEO
2. Then outside links to that page (not your homepage). Count the referring domains of the top 3 pages for the search, and aim for that range, not above it. When the gap between domains is out of reach, the gap between pages usually isn't
The prompt:
 
-> Big impressions on page two is a links job: links from your own pages first, then outside links to that URL, sized on what the top 3 have
Job 4: The patterns hiding in your queries
Open: Performance, Queries, last 3 months, then use the Query filter "Queries containing" with one word at a time: vs, alternative, free, best, review, price, near me, your city
This is where your next 20 pages come from. A pattern is one intent, one kind of page, many searches. "Tool vs tool". "Alternative to X". "Service in city". Once you've built the first page of a pattern, the next ones are much faster, and a young site can win them because the big sites don't bother
You'll often find that Google already shows you for searches you never targeted. That's Google telling you which patterns your site is credible for
The prompt:
 
Watch out for one thing: the "free" searches. They're less commercial, but the person typing them still has a problem and nothing to solve it with yet. Take them, and give the page its own reason to upgrade
-> Filter queries by one word at a time. The patterns Google already shows you for are your next pages
Job 5: The searches with no page
Open: Performance, Queries, then click a query and switch to the Pages tab
Sometimes a search sends impressions to a page that wasn't made for it: your homepage, a pricing page, an unrelated article. Google is trying to answer the search with what you have, and what you have is the wrong page type
That's a new-page decision, and the most common mistake is getting it wrong in one of two directions:
Creating a page that already exists. Before anything, check that no page of yours targets that intent already
Damaging a page that ranks to squeeze a new search in. Before you edit a page, list the queries it already gets clicks for. If the change risks them, create a new page instead
The prompt:
 
-> When Google answers a search with the wrong page, build the right one, without touching what already ranks
Job 6: The pages losing clicks
Open: Performance, Pages, Date, Compare, "last 3 months vs previous period", brand filtered out, sorted by the clicks difference
A page losing clicks has one of four stories. Find which one before you touch anything:
The search lost volume (seasonal, or the topic cooled). Check the impressions: down too? Nothing to fix
Someone overtook you. Impressions stable, position down. Read page one: what does the new winner do that you don't?
An AI Overview or a new box ate the click. Position stable, impressions stable, CTR down
Your page got worse, often after an edit. Check your log
Only the second and fourth stories call for work on the page. And if the page was edited less than 90 days ago, the answer is to wait: Google keeps a page in a transition of roughly three months after a real change, with a dip of about three weeks before it settles. Touching it again restarts the clock
The prompt:
 
-> A drop has a story: volume, a rival, a new result type, or your own edit. Only two of the four are worth touching the page for

Job 7: The zombie pages
Open: Performance, 16 months, Pages tab, brand filtered out, sorted by impressions ascending
At the bottom you'll find pages with almost no impressions in 16 months. They target no search, or one nobody types. They don't hurt you much individually, but they water down a site and waste the time Google spends on it
For each zombie, one of two decisions:
It has potential (a real search it could target, a business reason to exist): re-optimize it once, properly, around one keyword
It doesn't: delete it, and redirect it to the closest page if it ever had links or visits
Two guardrails. Never judge a page younger than 2 to 6 months: it hasn't had its chance. And never delete without a redirect plan for anything that has links pointing to it
The prompt:
 
-> Pages with no impressions in 16 months: re-optimize once or delete with a redirect. Never before 6 months of life
Job 8: The indexing check, money pages only
Open: Indexing, Pages
The indexing report is where people panic, and usually shouldn't
 
On chatseo.app, Google says 1.57K pages are not indexed and 262 are. Sounds terrible. It isn't, mostly. Redirects, alternate versions with a proper canonical, pages we excluded on purpose with noindex: that's normal housekeeping, and Google is doing exactly what we asked
The only question is: are the pages that make money, and the new pages you're betting on, indexed? Check those, one by one, with the URL Inspection tool at the top of Search Console
If a money page shows "Crawled, currently not indexed", a setting is rarely the cause. Most of the time the page adds too little that the top results don't already say, or no page of yours points at it. Run Job 3's internal links first, settings last
And skip the deep technical audit unless you run a huge site. WordPress, Shopify, Webflow and the modern frameworks handle the basics out of the box today. A full audit is one of the most common ways to burn a month and move nothing
The prompt:
 
-> Ignore the scary total. Check that the pages that sell are indexed, one by one
Job 9: The 90-day judge
Open: your change log, then Performance with an exact Page filter and an exact Query filter for each changed page
Every change you make gets a line in a log: the date, the page, the query it targets, the position and clicks that day, and a check date 90 days later
Then, every Monday, your head of SEO opens only the pages whose check date has come. Everything else is "not due"
This is the job that saves the most time, because it's the one that stops you working. Most SEO effort is wasted on pages that were changed recently and haven't settled yet. Google's own people put typical delays at about a day for a new URL to be discovered, a day or two for a title change to show, one to three weeks for a canonical, and three to six months to recover from a core update. A page needs its three months

-> Log every change with a check date 90 days out. Judge only what's due, and say "not due" to the rest
Job 10: The new-market scout
Open: Performance, Countries, last 3 months, brand filtered out
Look for countries where you get impressions without trying. A country that keeps showing up is Google telling you there's demand for what you do there
Before you open a new market, two rules:
Each country gets its own keyword research. People in different markets type different searches for the same need. A word-for-word translation of your pages targets searches nobody makes
The structure matters: one domain that isn't tied to a country (a .com), one folder per language, hreflang in both directions, the correct language set on each page, and a language switch that works
The prompt:
 
-> Countries that show up on their own are demand you didn't pay for. Research each market's own searches, never translate
What Search Console can't see
Your head of SEO has blind spots. Know them, so you don't make decisions on half the picture
Conversions. Search Console stops at the click. A page with 1,000 clicks that never converts is a problem to investigate, not a win to celebrate: wrong audience, or wrong offer. Join your clicks with your analytics or your signups before you call a page a success
AI answers. Search Console shows Google Search, and recently a separate view for Google's AI features. It doesn't show you what ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude or Gemini say about you
For those, three things work, and none of them costs much:
Bing Webmaster Tools has an AI Performance report, free, with citations and the actual searches the AI typed. It's the only real data on this I know of
Test 3 to 5 real questions a week, by hand, in a private window, the way a buyer would ask them. Note who gets named
Remember how those assistants work: they turn a question into searches and cite what ranks. So the work is mostly the same SEO you're already doing. The extra 10% is getting into other people's "best" lists, filling your review profiles, and showing up in the threads people read
What you don't need is a paid visibility score that tests prompts nobody types. On a small budget it's the worst option: expensive, and biased toward making you feel good or bad
Backlinks. Search Console's Links report shows some of your links, not all, and nothing about your competitors. When Job 3 says "links to that page", you'll need a backlink tool for the top 3's numbers
-> Join clicks to conversions, check AI answers by hand plus Bing's free AI report, and use a backlink tool for the competitors

Part 4. The same head of SEO, five kinds of site
The ten jobs don't change from one site to the next. What changes is which three matter most, and what your head of SEO should ignore. Here's how I'd brief it for the sites I see most often
A SaaS or a software tool
Your money is in three patterns: "alternative to [competitor]", "[competitor] vs [competitor]", and "[job] tool" or "[job] software". People typing them are choosing, which is as close to buying as search gets
So your Monday weighs Job 1, Job 3 and Job 4 above everything else. The blog matters less than you think. A guide on a broad topic brings readers who'll never pay, and Search Console will happily show you thousands of impressions on it, which is exactly why you need the brand filter and a list of money pages in HEAD.md
What to add to the job description:
 
That last line is from our own data. On chatseo.app, the "free [tool] alternative" pages are what bring the non-brand clicks, not the guides
A local business
A plumber, a dentist, a restaurant, a law firm. Here Search Console is half the picture, because a big share of the clicks happen in the map pack and on the Google Business Profile, which have their own stats
What your head of SEO watches:
Queries with a city or a neighborhood in them. Filter with the city name and read them as one pattern
Service pages, one per service, with the city in the title. "Emergency plumber in [city]" deserves its own URL, not a line on the homepage
Pages losing clicks after the season turns. Heating in October, air conditioning in May. Job 6 has to compare the same months of last year, not the last 3 months
What it should skip: content for people outside your area, and most of the blog. Five excellent service pages and a profile with real reviews beat fifty articles
An e-commerce store
The store is the one case where the technical side really deserves time, because faceted navigation can create hundreds of thousands of URLs Google has to crawl
So Job 8, the indexing check, moves up. But keep the same discipline: you check that the category pages and the best-selling product pages are indexed, one by one. The long "Excluded" list of filter combinations is usually Google doing its job
The jobs that pay most:
Job 3 on category pages. A category at position 14 for "[product type]" is worth more than a hundred product pages at position 40
Job 2 on titles. Product titles copied from the supplier's feed are the most common waste I see. The search people type is rarely the reference number
Job 7, the zombies. Out-of-stock products that will never come back should redirect to their closest category, not sit as thin pages forever
A content site or a blog that lives on ads or affiliation
Here impressions are the product, so the brand filter matters less, and Job 6 matters most. Traffic on a content site leaks slowly: an article that was at position 3 slides to 6, and the clicks halve before anyone notices
Your head of SEO's first question every Monday: which pages lost the most clicks against the same 4 weeks before, and did they lose position or did the search itself shrink? The two need opposite fixes. A lost position means a better page or more links. A shrinking search means you stop investing in it
The trap on content sites is updating everything all the time. Change the date and three sentences, and you've reset the 90-day clock for nothing. Update a page when Job 6 says it's losing, and log it
A client site, if you're an agency or a freelancer
The routine is the same, and it does something agencies rarely do: it gives the client a short list instead of a long report
Two things to add:
One HEAD.md per client, never a shared one. The money pages and the brand name are different every time, and mixing them is how an AI ends up recommending the wrong client's competitor
The client report is the Monday list itself, plus the 90-day verdicts that came due. "Here's what we changed, here's what we judged, here's what's not due yet." Clients understand that far better than a 30-page PDF
When I was freelance, the reports clients actually read were one page long. Everything else ended up in a folder nobody opened

The five numbers people misread in the report
Search Console is honest, but it's easy to read wrong. These are the five mistakes I see most, including from people who've done SEO for years
1. Reading the total and not the split. The headline curve mixes your name, your products and the searches you actually compete on. A brand campaign or a podcast appearance moves it, and you'll think your SEO improved. Always read the non-brand view before you conclude anything
2. Celebrating impressions. Impressions grow every time Google tests your page on a new search, often at position 50 where nobody scrolls. A jump in impressions with no movement in clicks usually means Google is exploring, not rewarding. It's a good sign, not a result
3. Panicking over CTR. Your click rate drops every time you start appearing on more searches, because the new ones come in low. Read CTR per query, at a fixed position range, or don't read it at all
4. Comparing the wrong periods. The last 28 days against the 28 before it is fine for a fast check, but a seasonal business needs the same weeks of last year. Otherwise every December looks like a disaster and every January like a miracle
5. Trusting the newest days. The last two or three days are incomplete. A cliff at the very end of the curve is almost always data that hasn't arrived yet. Wait, then look again
Put these five in the job description too, so your AI never reports a "drop" that's just Tuesday's missing data:
 
-> Split before you read, distrust impressions alone, read CTR per query, compare like with like, ignore the last 3 days

Your money pages list, done properly
Half of the jobs depend on one short list in HEAD.md: the pages that make you money. Most people write it from memory and get it wrong
Here's how to build it in ten minutes:
1. Open your analytics or your payment tool and list the pages people visit right before they sign up, book or buy. That's the real list, not the one you'd guess
2. Add the pages you'd defend if a competitor copied them: the product page, the pricing page, the two or three comparison pages
3. For each one, write the search it's meant to win, in the words a buyer types
4. Stop at 20. If you have more "money pages" than that, some of them aren't
The list changes what your AI does. Job 7 never proposes to delete a money page. Job 8 only checks those. Job 3 starts with them. And when a page on the list loses clicks, Job 6 flags it first, ahead of any blog post
Review the list every quarter, when the 90-day verdicts come in. A page that keeps winning clicks and never converts gets moved off it
When to overrule your head of SEO
Search Console knows Google. It doesn't know your business. Four times you should overrule what the data says:
The data points at a search your customers never make. A query can have thousands of impressions and be the wrong audience: students, job seekers, people looking for something with the same name. Strike it from the list
The quick win is on a page you're about to remove. A product you're discontinuing doesn't deserve new titles and links, even if it's sitting at position 6
The pattern is real but the page would be weak. If you have nothing better to say than the top 3, Job 4 can wait until you do. A thin page on a good pattern drags the pattern down
The numbers are too small to mean anything. Twelve impressions and a position change of two places is noise. Below roughly a hundred impressions a month, don't let the AI draw conclusions
Write these exceptions in HEAD.md too, as you meet them. After two months, the job description reflects your judgment, not just Google's numbers, and the AI's proposals get sharper every week

You can, and it saves the export step. ChatSEO does it, and there are other ways. But start with the exports for a few weeks. Choosing the filters yourself is how you learn what each job is asking, and you'll write a much better job description afterwards
"What if my site is brand new and has no data?"
Then the head of SEO has nothing to read yet, and that's fine. Publish your first pages on searches you can realistically win, submit the sitemap, and start the Monday meeting the week impressions appear. It's usually a matter of weeks. Until then, the work is the pages themselves
"How many queries should I give the AI?"
The top 30 to 50 after the filters, not the full export. A 1,000-row file makes the AI summarize instead of decide, and you can't check its answer
"Does this replace an SEO agency?"
It replaces the part most agencies bill for and rarely do well: reading the data every week and turning it into a short list. It doesn't replace the writing, the links or the judgment on what your buyers want. If you hire someone, hand them this routine and judge them on the change log, not on the report
"My numbers dropped right after I changed something. Should I undo it?"
Almost never in the first weeks. Rankings wobble after a change before they settle. Undo only if it was a mistake you can name, like a wrong canonical or a page set to noindex. Otherwise, write it in the log and let the 90 days run
Final advice
A few things I learned the hard way running this on real sites:
Keep the exports small. The AI does better with 30 filtered rows than 1,000 raw ones, and you can check 30 rows yourself
Never let the AI quote an average position without the exact filters. It's the number that misleads people most, and an AI will happily quote it with confidence
Write down the "why" of every change. In three months you won't remember whether you changed that title because of CTR or because of a new query, and the verdict depends on it
Don't confuse activity with progress. The most valuable output of the Monday meeting is often "not due" next to most of your pages
And ignore the advice to clean "toxic" backlinks, add an llms.txt for AI, or chase a keyword density. Google ignores junk links pointing at you (except after a manual action, which Search Console would show you), the AI assistants read the normal index, and density has never been the lever. Intent is
The limits, honestly
These numbers are chatseo.app's, read this morning. A different niche, a different authority, different competitors: your timing will differ, sometimes by a lot
Search Console data is sampled and delayed by a couple of days. It's the best free view of Google you'll get, not a perfect one
Positions move every week. A page at position 8 today can be at 12 next month without you touching it
Search Console tells you where to look. It doesn't write the page, earn the links or build the brand. Those are still the work
No one can promise a position. If someone swears you'll be on page one within a month, what they're really selling is the promise
